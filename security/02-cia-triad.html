Lesson 2 · Foundations

What security actually protects.

Ask three people what "secure" means and you'll get three answers. The field has a precise one. Everything you'll ever protect falls into exactly three buckets — and every attack is an assault on one of them.

~7 min · interactive · builds on Lesson 1

"Is this system secure?" is the wrong question, because "secure" isn't one thing. The right question is: secure against what? To answer that, security pros use a 3-part definition so old and so useful it has a nickname: the CIA triad. (No relation to the agency.)

The three things — defined

Confidentiality
Only the right people can read the data. Broken when secrets leak — a database dumped, a password sniffed, medical records exposed. The defense: encryption and access control.
Integrity
The data is correct and unaltered — nobody changed it without authorization. Broken when an attacker edits a bank balance, swaps a download for malware, or forges a message. The defense: hashing and digital signatures.
Availability
The system is there when you need it. Broken when a site is knocked offline — a DDoS flood, ransomware locking your files, a server crash. The defense: redundancy, backups, rate limiting.

Every security incident you'll ever read about attacks one or more of these. A data breach is a Confidentiality failure. A defaced website is Integrity. A site taken down by a flood is Availability. Naming which one is broken is the first move of every defender.

Thinking like an attacker: threat modeling

Defenders don't wait to be attacked — they sit down and ask, ahead of time, "if I wanted to break this, how would I?" That deliberate exercise is threat modeling, and it's the single most valuable habit in security. Four questions, in order:

1. What are we protecting? (assets)
The data and functions that matter — user passwords, payment info, the ability to stay online. You can't defend everything equally; name what's precious.
2. Who might attack it, and why? (threat actors)
A bored teenager, a criminal after money, a competitor, an insider, a nation-state. Their skill and motivation shape what defenses you need.
3. How could they get in? (attack surface)
Every door: the login page, an API, an employee's laptop, a third-party library, a phishable human. Lesson 1's "every handoff" — now you list them on purpose.
4. What will we do about each? (mitigations)
For each realistic path: block it, detect it, or accept the risk knowingly. You can't fix everything — you decide what's worth defending.

Amateurs ask "is it secure?" Defenders ask "what are we protecting, from whom, through which door, and what do we do about it?"

For your own projects

You build web apps (RoShop, Atlas). Threat-model one in four sentences: Asset = users' accounts and any payment flow. Actors = scammers, bored griefers. Surface = the login form, the admin page, the Firebase rules. Mitigation = strong auth, locked-down database rules, no secrets in client code. You just did real security work.

Recap

Security protects three things: Confidentiality (only the right people read it), Integrity (nobody alters it), Availability (it's there when needed). Every attack breaks at least one. And before you defend, you threat-model: what, from whom, through where, and what to do. Hold the triad and those four questions — they frame every lesson that follows.
