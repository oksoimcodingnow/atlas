Six steps, and at every single one a defense you learned could have ended the breach: MFA at the login, escaping at the comment box, least privilege on the server, locked-down cloud rules, encryption on the data, and logging to catch it. The attacker needed every link to hold. You only needed one. That asymmetry — many chances to defend, the attacker needs them all to fail — is the quiet reason most attacks lose. Now you can read a breach headline and see exactly where the chain broke.

What you actually have now

1The map. Packets, DNS, ports, HTTPS — you can picture what happens on the wire (L1), and you name the three things security protects: confidentiality, integrity, availability (L2).
2The primitives. Encryption vs hashing, public-key, salted slow-hashed passwords, MFA (L3–L4) — the toolbox under every padlock and login.
3The attacks & their fixes. Injection/XSS/CSRF, the wire, malware detection, phishing (L5–L8) — and the one rule under most of it: data is data, never code; never trust input; slow down on pressure.
4Systems at scale. Secure coding & OWASP, OS access control & least privilege, cloud misconfiguration & Firebase rules (L9–L11) — directly applicable to your own RoShop and Atlas.
5The profession. Defense in depth, assume-breach, the incident-response playbook, forensics (L12–L13) — how real defenders actually work.

This is the working mental model behind CompTIA Security+ and the foundation under CISSP and OSCP — not memorized trivia, but ideas you can reason from. More than that: you'll write safer code because you've seen the unsafe version broken, and you'll read the news about a breach and actually understand what happened, and where it could have been stopped.

Security isn't a wall. It's a chain of chances to defend — and the defender only needs one link to hold.

Where to go from here

Practice legally: TryHackMe and HackTheBox offer intentionally-vulnerable labs to practice on (the white-hat way — your own targets, with permission). Harden what you own: turn on MFA everywhere, audit your Firebase rules, never commit a secret. Keep the mindset: at every "place data changes hands," ask "is this trusted, and what's the minimum access needed?" That single habit is most of the craft.
