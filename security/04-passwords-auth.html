Lesson 4 · Foundations

Passwords, and why length wins.

The password is the oldest lock on the internet and the most attacked. Understanding how it's stored, how it's cracked, and what actually makes one strong is the most directly useful security knowledge you'll ever own — for your own accounts and the ones you build.

~8 min · interactive · builds on Lesson 3

From Lesson 3 you know passwords should be hashed, never encrypted or stored plain. But hashing alone isn't enough — attackers have clever ways around a bare hash. Let's see the full picture, then watch how fast different passwords fall.

How storage actually works (and fails)

plain text — the cardinal sin
Storing the literal password. One database breach and every account is instantly owned. Still happens. Inexcusable.
bare hash — better, but beatable
Store hash(password). Now a breach doesn't reveal passwords directly. But attackers precompute hashes of millions of common passwords (a rainbow table) and just look yours up. And two users with the same password get the same hash — visible at a glance.
salt — the fix
Add a unique random value to each password before hashing: hash(password + salt). Now every stored hash is unique even for identical passwords, and precomputed tables are useless (they'd need to be rebuilt per salt). The salt is stored alongside — it's not secret, it just has to be unique.
slow hashing — the final layer
Use a function deliberately built to be slow (bcrypt, scrypt, argon2). A defender hashes once per login — no problem. An attacker trying billions of guesses is throttled to a crawl. Slowness is a feature here.

How attackers guess

brute force
Try every possible combination. Beaten purely by length — each extra character multiplies the search space.
dictionary attack
Try known words, names, and leaked passwords first. This is why P@ssw0rd1 is weak despite the symbols — it's in every list. Predictability, not character types, is the enemy.
credential stuffing
Take passwords leaked from one breached site and try them on others, betting people reuse. This is why reuse is the real killer — one breach becomes all your accounts.

Beyond passwords: MFA

Even a perfect password can be phished or leaked. The answer is to need more than one factor:

multi-factor authentication (MFA / 2FA)
Require two of: something you know (password), something you have (your phone, a hardware key), something you are (fingerprint, face). A stolen password alone is now useless — the attacker also needs your physical phone. This single setting blocks the overwhelming majority of account takeovers.
password manager
The real-world answer to "long, unique, never-reused, for every site": a tool that generates and remembers them so you don't have to. You memorize one strong master phrase; it handles the rest.
Do this today

Turn on MFA for your email and GitHub — your email is the master key that resets every other account. Use a password manager so every site gets a unique password (kills credential stuffing). And when you build a login: salt + slow-hash (bcrypt/argon2), never plain, never bare, never encrypted.

Recap

Store passwords salted and slow-hashed, never plain or reversible. Attackers use brute force (beaten by length), dictionaries (beaten by unpredictability), and credential stuffing (beaten by never reusing). Length beats complexity because it's exponential. And MFA makes a stolen password nearly worthless — the highest-value security habit you have.
