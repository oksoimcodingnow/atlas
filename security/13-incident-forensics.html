Lesson 13 · The profession

When (not if) it happens.

Every defender eventually faces a real breach. What separates a contained incident from a catastrophe isn't luck — it's having a plan and following it in order. Panic improvises; professionals run the playbook.

~8 min · interactive · builds on Lesson 12 · the assume-breach payoff

Lesson 12 said: assume breach. This is what you do when it comes true. Incident response (IR) is the disciplined process of handling a breach, and digital forensics is reconstructing what happened. Both reward calm order over heroics — and there's a famous six-step sequence, in a specific order, for a reason.

The IR playbook — defined

1. Preparation
Before anything happens: have a plan, backups, logging, and contacts ready. The work you do now decides how the bad day goes. The only step that happens before the breach.
2. Identification
Detect and confirm: is this real? What's affected? Your logging/SIEM (Lesson 12) is what makes this possible — you can't respond to what you can't see.
3. Containment
Stop the bleeding: isolate affected systems so it can't spread, without destroying evidence. Pull the network cable, not the power (powering off can wipe memory evidence — forensics needs it).
4. Eradication
Remove the threat: delete the malware, close the hole they came through, revoke stolen credentials. Don't just clean up — fix the way in, or they'll be back tonight.
5. Recovery
Restore systems to normal — from clean backups — and watch closely to be sure the threat is truly gone before declaring victory.
6. Lessons learned
The step everyone skips and shouldn't. A blameless post-mortem: how did it happen, what would have stopped it, what do we change? This feeds back into Preparation. The incident only pays off if you learn from it.

The order matters: run the response

A breach just hit. You'll be offered the next action at each step — pick the right one, in the right order. Getting the sequence wrong is how real responses go bad.

The forensics side — defined

chain of custody
A documented record of who handled each piece of evidence, when, and how — so it holds up if it's ever needed legally. Break the chain and the evidence is worthless in court.
order of volatility
Collect the most fragile evidence first: live memory (RAM) vanishes on shutdown, network connections close, disk is more durable. This is exactly why "pull the network, not the power" — powering off destroys the volatile evidence.
indicators of compromise (IOCs)
The fingerprints a breach leaves: a malicious file hash, a strange IP, an odd registry key. Investigators use them to scope the damage and to detect the same attacker elsewhere.

A breach is a fire. You don't debate firefighting philosophy in the smoke — you run the drill you practiced when the air was clear.

The one habit that matters most

Almost everything that goes wrong in a real incident traces back to Preparation — no plan, no backups, no logs to investigate with, no idea who to call. You can't improvise forensics or restore from a backup you never made. The boring work done on a calm day is what saves you on the worst one. (And "lessons learned" is how each breach makes the next one less likely.)

Recap

Breaches are when, not if. Incident response runs a six-step playbook in order — prepare, identify, contain, eradicate, recover, learn — and the order matters (contain before you eradicate; preserve evidence before you wipe). Forensics reconstructs the attack via chain of custody, order of volatility, and IOCs. The whole thing rests on Preparation: the calm-day work that turns a catastrophe into a contained incident.
