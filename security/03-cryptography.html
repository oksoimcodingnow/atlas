Lesson 3 · Foundations

Cryptography you can feel.

Crypto sounds like impenetrable math. The core ideas aren't. Three of them — encryption, hashing, and keys — carry almost all of practical security, and once you can tell them apart, half of what's confusing becomes obvious.

~8 min · interactive · builds on Lessons 1–2

Lesson 1 ended with a problem: plain HTTP is readable by everyone on the path. Cryptography is the fix — but "encryption" gets used as a fuzzy magic word. Let's make it sharp. There are two completely different tools people lump together, and confusing them is the #1 beginner mistake.

Encryption vs hashing — the crucial split

encryption — reversible, for secrecy
Scramble data so only someone with the key can unscramble it. It's a two-way door: encrypt with the key, decrypt with the key. Used to keep things secret in transit and at rest. The message survives — it's just hidden.
hashing — one-way, for integrity
Run data through a function that produces a fixed-size fingerprint, and cannot be reversed. There's no "un-hash." The same input always gives the same fingerprint; any tiny change gives a wildly different one. Used to verify nothing was altered, and to store passwords (Lesson 4).
The mistake to never make

"The passwords were encrypted" is what breached companies say when they mean hashed — or worse, when they really did encrypt (reversible!) and the attacker got the key too. Passwords should be hashed, not encrypted. If you can decrypt it back to the original, so can the attacker who steals your key.

Symmetric vs public-key — the two kinds of encryption

symmetric encryption
One shared key locks and unlocks. Fast, simple — but both sides need the same secret key, and getting it to each other safely is the hard part. (Algorithm you'll hear: AES.)
public-key (asymmetric) encryption
A pair of keys: a public one you hand out freely, and a private one you guard. Anything locked with the public key can only be opened with the private one. This solves the "how do we share a key" problem — you can receive secrets from strangers without ever exchanging a secret first. (Algorithms: RSA, ECC.) It's what makes HTTPS, signatures, and the padlock possible.
digital signature
Public-key, run in reverse: sign with your private key, and anyone can verify with your public key. It proves who sent something and that it wasn't altered — integrity + authenticity in one move.

Encryption hides a message and can give it back. Hashing fingerprints a message and never gives it back. Different tools, different jobs — mix them up and you build the next breach.

Where you've already met this

The HTTPS padlock from Lesson 1 is all three at once: public-key crypto to safely agree on a shared key, symmetric encryption (fast) for the actual data, and signatures to prove the server is really who it claims. When you download a file and the site lists a "SHA-256 checksum," that's a hash — you re-hash your download and compare, to prove it wasn't tampered with in transit (an integrity check from Lesson 2).

Recap

Encryption is reversible and needs a key — for secrecy. Hashing is one-way with no key — for integrity. Encryption comes in symmetric (one shared key, fast) and public-key (a public/private pair that lets strangers send you secrets and lets you sign things). These primitives, combined, are the entire toolbox behind HTTPS, signatures, and safe password storage.
