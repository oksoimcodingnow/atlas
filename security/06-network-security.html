Lesson 6 · Attacks & defenses

Defending the wire.

Lesson 1 showed your data hopping across networks as readable postcards. This lesson is the defender's answer: the tools that decide who gets in, who can listen, and how to send a secret across a wire full of strangers.

~8 min · interactive · builds on Lessons 1 & 3

Every connection crosses machines you don't control — your router, your ISP, the coffee shop's access point, dozens of hops. Network security is the discipline of staying safe anyway. Three jobs: control who can reach you (firewalls), hide what you send (encryption/VPN), and verify who you're talking to (TLS).

The defender's toolkit — defined

firewall
A gatekeeper that decides which traffic is allowed in or out, by rules (port, address, protocol). The core principle: default-deny — block everything, then open only the exact doors you need. An unused open port is an unlocked door nobody's watching.
TLS (the S in HTTPS)
The encryption tunnel that wraps your web traffic. It does two things at once: encrypts the data (confidentiality) and verifies the server's identity with a certificate (authenticity), using the public-key crypto from Lesson 3. The padlock means both held.
VPN (virtual private network)
An encrypted tunnel from your device to a trusted server, so everything you send is hidden from the local network (the coffee shop, the airport). Useful on untrusted Wi-Fi. Note: it moves your trust to the VPN provider — it doesn't make you anonymous or invincible.
man-in-the-middle (MITM)
An attacker who sits between you and the server, reading or altering traffic. On open HTTP they read everything. TLS is what defeats it — the certificate check means an impostor can't pretend to be your bank without a certificate they can't forge.
network segmentation
Splitting a network into zones so a breach in one can't reach the rest. Your IoT gadgets on one segment, your laptop on another — if the smart bulb gets popped, it can't see your files. The blast-radius limiter.

Eavesdropper's-eye view

You can't trust the network. So you stop relying on it: encrypt the data, verify the other end, and assume someone is always listening.

The practical takeaways

1. The padlock (HTTPS) is non-negotiable — it's what makes hostile Wi-Fi survivable. 2. Even with HTTPS, the network can see which sites you visit (the domain), just not the contents — a VPN hides that too. 3. "The Wi-Fi has a password" protects the network from outsiders joining; it does not encrypt your traffic from others already on it. Different things.

Recap

You never control the wire, so you defend regardless. Firewalls default-deny and open only needed ports. TLS/HTTPS encrypts and authenticates — the answer to man-in-the-middle. VPNs tunnel you off untrusted Wi-Fi (moving trust to the provider). Segmentation limits the blast radius. The mindset: assume the network is hostile, and make it not matter.
