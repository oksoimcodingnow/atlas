What is a stock worth?

Price is what the market quotes. Value is what the business is actually worth. This is how you estimate the second one — and why the gap between them is where investors make money.

Price is not valuethe whole idea

A stock is a claim on a business's future cash. Its intrinsic value is what all that future cash is worth today. The price is just the last number two strangers agreed on. They drift apart constantly — fear, hype, indexes, headlines.

Investing is buying when price < value by a wide enough margin that you're protected even if your estimate is off. That cushion has a name:

Margin of safety

If you think a stock is worth $100, you don't pay $98 — you wait for $70. The 30% gap is your protection against being wrong, against bad luck, against the future not cooperating. Valuation gives you the $100 estimate; the margin of safety admits the estimate is fuzzy.

There are two honest ways to estimate value: build it up from the cash a business will produce (DCF, absolute), or compare it to what similar businesses sell for (multiples, relative). Good analysts use both and see if they agree.

Discounted cash flowvalue from first principles

A business is worth the cash it will hand you over its life — but a dollar in 10 years is worth less than a dollar today, so you discount future cash back to the present. Sum the discounted free cash flows, add a terminal value for everything past your forecast:

V = ∑t=1..n FCFt(1 + WACC)t + TV(1 + WACC)n

Three pieces do the work:

1. Free cash flow (FCF) — the cash a business throws off after running and reinvesting in itself. The thing you actually own a share of.

2. The discount rate (WACC) — the return you require, blending the cost of equity and debt. Cost of equity comes from CAPM (your formula sheet): re = Rf + β(Rm − Rf). Riskier business → higher WACC → lower value.

3. Terminal value — the business doesn't stop at year n. The Gordon-growth form assumes cash grows forever at a slow rate g (near GDP, 2–4%):

TV = FCFn · (1 + g)WACC − g

Here's the thing nobody tells beginners: the terminal value is usually 60–80% of the whole answer. Most of a DCF's "value" lives in assumptions about a future you can't see. Don't take my word for it — drag the sliders below and watch.

Example

Target earns $3.00/share. Peers trade at P/E of 15, 17, 18, 19, 22 → median 18. Implied price = 3.00 × 18 = $54. If it trades at $45, it looks ~17% cheap — but first ask why: slower growth, thinner margins, or more risk can justify the discount. A low multiple is a question, not an answer.

The trapswhere valuations lie

Same spirit as the rest of your quant work — a model is only as honest as its inputs:

Terminal value dominates. 60–80% of a DCF is the perpetuity. A tiny change in g or WACC swings the answer wildly (you just saw it). Treat the output as a range, not a number.

Garbage in, garbage out. A DCF gives a precise-looking number from guessed inputs. False precision → false confidence. Always run the assumptions up and down (sensitivity).

Growth forever is a fantasy. No company compounds at 15% indefinitely — competition pulls it back to the mean. Fade high growth toward GDP.

A cheap multiple can be a trap. Markets aren't dumb; a low P/E often prices in real deterioration. Cheap and broken is not a bargain.

The honest workflow

Build a DCF for a value range. Cross-check with peer multiples. Demand a margin of safety between price and your estimate. If the two methods disagree wildly, you've found the question worth researching — not a verdict to act on.
