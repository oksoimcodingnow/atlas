Win your first case competition.

A practical playbook for a brand-new team: what it is, how the day runs, who does what, and the small number of habits that actually decide who wins.

Written for a first-timer. Skip nothing on the first read; later, you'll use it as a checklist. The core truth: a clear, well-rehearsed 7/10 idea beats a brilliant 9/10 idea presented as mush.

01What it actually is

You and 3–4 teammates become consultants for a day. You're handed a real-ish business problem (a company facing a decision: should they expand? launch? cut a product? fix declining sales?), given a few hours, and you must deliver a recommendation to a panel of judges, then defend it under questioning.

It is not an exam with a right answer. Two teams can recommend opposite things and both score well — because judges grade your reasoning and delivery, not whether you guessed "the answer." Your job is to make a clear call and defend it convincingly.

It's a consulting job interview disguised as a contest. They're watching how you think and how you persuade.

03Who does what (team roles)

A 4-person team works best when roles are explicit. You can flex, but name them up front so nothing falls through the cracks:

  • The Lead / synthesizer — keeps the team pointed at the core question, makes the final call when you're split, and owns the storyline. Stops rabbit-holes.
  • The Numbers person — owns the financials: market sizing, the one key calculation, the ROI/breakeven. You're a finance-engineering student — this is your natural seat, and it's a real edge.
  • The Slide builder — turns the thinking into a clean, readable deck while others analyze. Starts early; an ugly rushed deck loses points.
  • The Presenter(s) — usually 2 people split the talking. Confident, clear, rehearsed. Everyone should be ready to field a Q&A question in their area.
For a new team specifically
  • Play a practice case together before the real one. The single biggest gap for new teams isn't intelligence — it's never having run the clock as a unit. Do one full timed dry-run on an old case. It will feel chaotic; that's the point of doing it before it counts.
  • Diversity helps. Different majors/perspectives catch issues a homogeneous team misses. A finance person + a marketing person + an ops thinker reads a case more completely.
04The framework (structure beats brilliance)

Judges follow a clear structure; they get lost in scattered genius. Use a known framework as your skeleton — it signals "we think like consultants." The reliable spine:

Problem → Options → Recommendation → Why (with a number) → Risks → Next steps.

Inside that spine, pull in the classic tools where they earn their place (don't dump all of them):

  • SWOT — strengths/weaknesses/opportunities/threats. Fast situational read.
  • Porter's Five Forces — industry competitiveness (suppliers, buyers, new entrants, substitutes, rivalry). Use when the question is "should we enter / compete here?"
  • PESTEL — macro factors (political, economic, social, tech, environmental, legal). Use for market-entry or big-picture context.
  • Profit/value-driver tree — break revenue and cost into pieces to find what actually moves the needle. The numbers person's best friend.

Be hypothesis-driven: form a view early ("we think they should X"), then test it with analysis, rather than analyzing everything and hoping an answer appears. That's how real consultants — and winning teams — work.

05The deck (recommendation first)

The single most common mistake is building up to the recommendation at the end like a mystery novel. Judges want the answer on slide one. A winning structure:

  • Slide 1 — Executive summary. Your recommendation + the 2–3 reasons + the expected impact (ideally a number). A judge who only sees this slide should already know your whole argument.
  • The problem, framed. Show you understood the real question, broken into its key sub-issues.
  • The analysis. 2–3 slides of evidence — your framework, your numbers, the value drivers. Every slide answers "so what?"
  • The recommendation, in detail. What exactly to do, with a rough plan/timeline.
  • Risks & mitigations. Naming what could go wrong (and how you'd handle it) is a maturity signal that separates top teams.
  • Next steps. The concrete first actions. Always end on "here's what the client does Monday."
What makes a slide good
  • One message per slide, stated in the title as a full sentence ("Expanding to X adds ฿Y in 2 years"), not a topic label ("Expansion").
  • One chart or one number that carries the point. Judges remember the team that quantified.
  • The "so what" test: if a slide doesn't drive a decision, cut it.
06Q&A — where it's won or lost

Most teams over-build the deck and under-rehearse the defense. But judges often decide here, because Q&A reveals whether you actually understand your own analysis. How to handle it:

  • Stay consistent. Don't abandon your recommendation the moment a judge pushes. Defend it with reasoning. Conviction (backed by logic) scores; flip-flopping kills.
  • Pause, then answer the question asked. A two-second think beats a rushed ramble. Answer directly first, then support.
  • "That's a great question, and here's how we thought about it…" buys a beat and frames your answer.
  • If you don't know: say what you'd need to find out and how you'd get it. Honesty + a method beats bluffing — judges can smell a bluff.
  • Pre-assign questions. Before you present, brainstorm the 5 hardest questions and decide who answers each. The numbers person takes financial pushback, etc.
07The traps that sink new teams
Avoid these
  • No clear recommendation. Listing options and trailing off is the #1 new-team failure. Commit to one.
  • Analysis with no decision. A beautiful SWOT that doesn't lead anywhere is wasted. Every analysis must point to your call.
  • Zero numbers. "It will grow the market" means nothing. "It adds an estimated ฿120M over 3 years" lands. Quantify one thing well.
  • Over-building, never rehearsing. A polished, on-time, well-rehearsed presentation beats deeper analysis delivered rushed and shaky — every single time.
  • Ignoring risks. Pretending your plan is flawless reads as naïve. Naming the risk (and a mitigation) reads as mature.
  • Going over time. Judges cut you off; you lose your conclusion. Rehearse to land 30 seconds early.
  • One person dominates. Judges want to see a team. Share the airtime.

09Your prep checklist
Before competition day
  • Run ≥1 full timed practice case as a team (old cases are everywhere online; even a Harvard/Ivey case PDF works). The reps matter more than any reading.
  • Learn 3 frameworks cold — SWOT, Porter's Five Forces, a profit-driver tree. Don't memorize ten; know a few deeply.
  • Build a slide template in advance — clean exec-summary layout, chart styles, your team's colors. Don't design under the clock.
  • Practice fast market-sizing — "estimate the Thai market for X" on a napkin. This one skill wins Q&As.
  • Assign roles and agree on the prep-time split (read / analyze / build / rehearse). Write it down.
  • Pick a "decision deadline" — a time by which you MUST have committed to a recommendation, so you don't analyze forever.

The team that rehearses a clear, confident recommendation beats the team with more ideas and no time left to practice. Every time.
