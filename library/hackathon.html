Your first AI × Finance hackathon

The Hackathon playbook.

A case competition asks “what should they do?” A hackathon asks “what can you build?” — and you answer with a working thing. Here’s how a first-timer wins, especially this one.

Written for the CFA Society Thailand AI × Finance Hackathon — but the principles hold for any. Pair it with the live command center for dates, your roster, and the checklist.

01What a hackathon actually is

A hackathon is a build sprint: a theme is announced, you get a fixed window (sometimes 24–48 hours straight, sometimes a few weeks part-time), and you ship a working prototype — usually an app, a website, or a tool — then demo it to judges. You are not graded on a report. You are graded on: does it work, is the idea good, and can you show it convincingly.

The single most important truth, and the one beginners get wrong:

A winning hackathon project is not the most complex one. It’s a small thing that actually works, solving a real problem, shown well.

Teams that win pick the right problem, scope ruthlessly, and deliver a demo that makes judges take notice. Teams that lose dream big and have nothing that runs when the clock hits zero.

This particular event is a hybrid: the deliverable is a slide presentation, not 48 hours of non-stop code. That means your case-comp muscles — framing a problem, defending a recommendation, presenting — transfer directly. It’s a hackathon wearing a case-comp costume. That should lower your fear.

03How to spend the clock

The proven time split that winners use — read the judging rubric first, then:

Validate the idea (~15% of time)
Define the target user and the one problem. Check what already exists. Do not write a single line of code until the idea is validated.
Build the MVP (~65%)
Heads-down. Build the smallest version that demonstrates the idea end-to-end. Cut everything that isn’t the core “wow”.
Polish + demo prep (~20%)
Make it presentable, fix the demo path, build the slides, record a backup video, and rehearse the pitch out loud.
The rule that saves you
  • Read the judging criteria before you build. Teams that optimize for the rubric win more than teams that build what merely excites them.
  • Build toward the demo from hour one — every feature should be something you’ll show.
04Pick a winnable idea

A good hackathon idea is narrow, real, and demoable. For an AI × Finance theme, the sweet spot is: a specific finance pain + AI that visibly removes it + a number you can point to.

The test for a good idea

  • Can you demo it in 2 minutes? If the value only shows after 10 steps, it’s too big.
  • Is the problem real to a finance person? Judges here are CFA Charterholders — pick a pain they recognize.
  • Does AI do something a spreadsheet can’t? Summarize a 200-page filing, flag a risk in plain language, draft an analysis. Don’t use AI as decoration.
  • Can you tie it to impact? “Cuts research time from 3 hours to 5 minutes.” A number makes it land.
Example shapes (don’t copy — calibrate)
  • An assistant that reads a company’s annual report and answers an analyst’s questions with citations.
  • A tool that turns messy financial data into a one-page risk summary.
  • A “junior analyst” that drafts a first-cut valuation and explains its assumptions.

One sharp idea you can finish beats three clever ideas you can’t.

05Assign roles before you start

Diverse skills win: building, design, pitching, project management. For an AI project specifically, a subject-matter expert on the team gets you a long way ahead — and on a finance hackathon, that expert is you.

SeatOwns
Finance lead (you)Is the problem real? Is the impact number honest? The credibility in Q&A. Your edge — own it.
BuilderDrives the prototype (with Claude). Keeps scope small enough to finish.
Designer / slidesMakes the demo and deck clean. One message per slide.
PresenterTells the story in the pitch. Can be you if you’re comfortable — finance + delivery is a strong combo.

Roles can overlap on a 3–5 person team — the point is that someone owns each, so nothing falls through at 2am.

06Claude as your build-partner

This is your unfair advantage. Most teams have one stressed coder. You have a tireless pair-programmer that explains as it goes — and the workshop literally teaches Claude & Codex, so you walk in fluent in tools others are just meeting.

  • Make Claude scope, not just code. Ask “what’s the smallest version that demos the idea?” before building — it’s great at cutting ambition down to finishable.
  • Build in thin slices. One working feature at a time, verified, before the next. (Exactly how Atlas itself is built.)
  • Have it write your demo script + slide outline too — recommendation first, then how, then the live demo.
  • Let it explain the code to you so you can answer “how does it work?” in Q&A. Judges respect a team that understands its own build.
Honest caution
  • AI writes plausible-looking code that can be subtly wrong. Test the demo path repeatedly — don’t trust “it should work.”
  • Don’t let AI invent finance facts. You’re the expert; you verify every number.
07The demo — where it’s won

Judges want to understand your problem in the first 30 seconds, see a working demo (not slides about what you planned), and hear why it matters. A clean three-minute pitch with a live walkthrough beats a technically impressive project explained badly — every time.

The pitch shape

The problem (30 sec)
Who hurts, and how much. Make the judge feel it.
The solution, live (90 sec)
Show it working. Real screen, real result. The heart of the pitch.
Why it matters (40 sec)
The impact number + why AI is the right tool here.
Close (20 sec)
What’s next if you had more time. Confident, not apologetic.
The pro move everyone forgets
  • Record a backup video of the working app. Wifi drops and APIs fail during judging — a pre-recorded demo means a crash can’t sink you.
  • Rehearse out loud, with a timer, at least twice. Reading it in your head is not rehearsing.
08Traps that sink new teams
Avoid these
  • Over-scoping. The #1 killer. You’ll feel ambitious at hour 2 and desperate at hour 20. Cut early, cut hard.
  • Building before validating. A day spent building the wrong thing can’t be refunded.
  • Ignoring the rubric. Build for how you’re scored, not for what’s fun.
  • A demo that needs explaining. If you have to narrate why it’s cool, it isn’t showing it.
  • No backup. Live demo only → one glitch and you’re done.
  • AI as decoration. Bolting “AI” on for buzz. Judges (CFA pros) see through it. Make AI do real work.
  • Silent team. Roles undefined → two people build the same thing, one builds nothing.
09Why THIS hackathon fits you

Honest read: if someone designed a competition for your exact strengths, it would look like this one.

  • AI × Finance = your two edges on one table. Finance reasoning + AI building, together — most events reward only one.
  • They teach Claude & Codex in the workshop — you’re already fluent. You start ahead.
  • The output is a slide presentation — your case-comp skills transfer; it’s gentler than a 48-hour code sprint.
  • It’s the first ever in Thailand — everyone’s figuring it out together; lower floor, higher prestige to win.
  • 3–5 people, cross-institution, ม.ปลาย–ป.เอก — flexible team; your case-comp friends qualify.
Remember the two sentences
  • Case comp = commit to an answer.
  • Hackathon = finish something small that works, and show it well.

The scary part is the unknown. You now have the map. Go register, lock the team, and treat the workshop as a checkpoint to reach — not optional.
