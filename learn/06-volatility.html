Lesson 6 · The real edge

Volatility: the thing you can predict.

Lesson 5 ended on a claim: you can't predict whether a price goes up or down, but you can predict how wild tomorrow will be. This lesson proves it, and shows the models that do it.

~9 min · builds on Lesson 5 · with a volatility-clustering simulator

Direction is a coin flip. Size isn't.

Ask "will the price be higher tomorrow?" and the honest answer is roughly 50/50. We tested it: a real model on daily Bitcoin got ~49.5% accuracy. A coin flip. Markets price in what's knowable, so the next move is mostly noise.

But ask a different question, "how big will tomorrow's move be, up or down?", and suddenly there's structure. Big days cluster. A violent day tends to be followed by another violent day; a calm stretch stays calm. That clustering is volatility, and unlike direction, it's forecastable.

You can't say which way. You can often say how much.
volatility
How much returns swing around, usually the standard deviation of returns. Not direction, just magnitude of movement.
volatility clustering
The empirical fact that big moves follow big moves and calm follows calm. The whole reason vol is predictable.
EWMA
Exponentially-weighted moving average. A simple vol forecast that weights recent days more. RiskMetrics' classic: 94% weight on yesterday's estimate, 6% on yesterday's squared return.
GARCH(1,1)
A vol model that adds mean reversion: today's vol = a baseline + a piece of yesterday's shock + a piece of yesterday's vol. It pulls back toward a long-run level.

Do the real models beat a dumb guess?

The honest test (we ran it on Bitcoin): forecast tomorrow's variance, then compare to what actually happened, on data the model never saw. The bar to clear is a dumb baseline, "tomorrow's vol = the recent average." Beating zero is easy; beating the dumb baseline is the real test.

The result: both EWMA and GARCH beat the baseline out-of-sample, by a modest but real margin (GARCH tracked the changes best, ~0.23 correlation with next-day moves). Small, but genuine, and the opposite of the direction result, which was nothing.

Why this matters

Risk is the one thing you can see coming. That's why everything practical is built on it: position sizing (bet less when vol is high), options pricing (vol IS the price), risk limits, and the vol-targeting overlays that keep a portfolio's risk roughly constant. You forecast the storm, not the direction of the wind.

The trap

"Predictable" is not "huge." The edge over the dumb baseline was ~1%. And we found it on one asset, one period. Before trusting it: re-test on another market and timeframe. Vol is the most honest signal in markets, but it's still small, and small edges die fast when you stop checking.

A stock just had three huge down-days in a row. What does a volatility model predict for tomorrow?

You can forecast risk, not direction.

That single asymmetry is what real quant is built on. Next: a cautionary tale about a signal that looked real and wasn't.
