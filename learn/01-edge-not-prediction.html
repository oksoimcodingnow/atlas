Lesson 1 · Foundations

Edge, not prediction.

You don't get rich predicting the future. You get rich being a little less wrong than the person on the other side of the trade — over and over.

~6 min · anchored to your MM2 trading · interactive

When you traded MM2 knives, did you predict the exact future price of a Chroma? No. You knew when something was mispriced — a panicked seller dumping a Godly too cheap, or hype about to spike demand. You didn't see the future. You knew value better than the person across the trade.

That's the whole game in quant. And it's the thing beginners get exactly backwards.

The trap

Beginners think quant means "predict tomorrow's BTC price." That's nearly impossible, and it's the wrong frame. Day to day, BTC's direction is close to a coin flip — roughly 50/50 up or down. Smart, funded people have hunted that prediction for decades. If it were easy, it would already be gone.

↓

So when a model predicts direction with 70% accuracy in a backtest, the honest reaction isn't "I'm rich." It's "where's my bug?" — because that number is almost always a leak or an overfit. (We'll learn to hunt those.)

What a real edge looks like

A real edge is tiny. 52, maybe 53% instead of 50. Sounds useless. It isn't — and you already know why from MM2.

You didn't get rich on one god-tier flip. You got there by making many slightly-favorable trades and letting them stack. A 3% edge, sized right and repeated thousands of times, compounds into real money.

A small, real, repeatable edge — sized correctly — beats a big edge you can't trust.

Notice three things as you drag:

At 50%, the expected line is flat — pure noise. Some runs end up, some down, all luck, no edge. At 53%, the bold line tilts up and the random runs cluster around it: a tiny edge, made visible by repetition. Below 50%, you bleed — slowly, but surely. That is what a casino is: a 51% edge, repeated a million times.

The rule that saves you money

When our ML model later shows 51% out-of-sample accuracy, a beginner throws it away, disappointed. You'll know better: 51% that survives honest testing can be tradeable. And 68% that looks gorgeous is almost certainly a lie you need to catch.

The skill was never building the model. It's knowing which number to believe.

The recap

Quant is not fortune-telling. It's finding a small, real, repeatable statistical advantage — and sizing it so the math compounds in your favor. You've done this for six years with knives. Now we do it with code, and we learn to prove the edge is real before trusting a baht to it.
