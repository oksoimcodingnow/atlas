Lesson 3 · The big trap

How backtests lie.

Give a smart person a dataset and a few hours, and they can produce a backtest that looks like a money printer and means absolutely nothing. This lesson is how to not be that person.

~7 min · interactive · the most important lesson here

You felt this one already. Your SMA(25/30) crossover had an in-sample Sharpe of 1.5 — gorgeous. Out-of-sample it collapsed to −0.25. The backtest didn't lie by accident; backtests lie by default, and you have to actively fight it.

The terms, defined

in-sample / out-of-sample
In-sample is the data you used to build and tune the strategy. Out-of-sample (OOS) is fresh data it has never seen. Performance in-sample is almost meaningless; only OOS counts. It's the difference between acing a test you wrote yourself and acing one you didn't.
overfitting
When your model memorizes the noise in the past instead of the real pattern. An overfit strategy fits history perfectly and predicts the future terribly. The more knobs you tune, the easier it is to do by accident.
look-ahead bias
Accidentally using information you couldn't have had at the time — like using today's closing price to decide a trade you'd have to make before the close. It makes a strategy look psychic. The #1 silent bug in quant code.
data leakage
The broader family of look-ahead: any way future information sneaks into a past decision. In ML it's everywhere and devastating.
walk-forward validation
The cure. Split your history in time: tune on the early part, test on the later part it never saw. If the edge survives the unseen part, it might be real. You built exactly this in walk_forward.py.
data snooping / p-hacking
Trying 500 strategies, keeping the one that looked best, and reporting only that. With enough tries, something looks brilliant by pure luck. The fix: count your attempts, and distrust the winner.

Watch the two error numbers. Train error always falls as complexity rises — the curve hugs the dots harder. But test error makes a U: it drops, hits a sweet spot, then climbs as the model starts memorizing noise it can't generalize. The backtest only shows you the train number. Reality grades you on the test one.

Any strategy can be made to look perfect on the past. The only question that matters is whether it works on data it has never seen.

The discipline that protects you

1. Hold out the future. Never tune on the data you test on. 2. Count your attempts. The 30th strategy you tried "winning" is probably luck. 3. Shift your signals. Trade on yesterday's close, never today's — that one .shift(1) is the difference between a real edge and look-ahead fantasy. 4. Distrust beauty. A Sharpe over ~3 or accuracy over ~60% on price direction is a bug until proven otherwise.

Recap

The past can be fit perfectly and predict nothing. Overfitting memorizes noise; look-ahead bias steals from the future; data snooping rewards luck. Walk-forward validation is your defense — and a strategy that survives it is worth more than a hundred that only shine in-sample.
