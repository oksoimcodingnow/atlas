Lesson 7 · Don't fool yourself

The false-positive trap.

We went hunting for a real-world signal: does weather in the Brazilian coffee belt predict coffee prices? One result looked spectacular. It was a complete illusion. Here's how the illusion forms, and how to kill it.

~8 min · a true story from this project · with a p-value illusion demo

The seductive find

The idea is sensible: drought hurts the harvest, so dry weather should push coffee prices up months later. We pulled 20 years of real data and tested it. One feature, rainfall vs the seasonal normal, lit up: a correlation of −0.21 with prices three months later, with a p-value of 0.0. The sign was even economically right. It looked like a discovery.

It wasn't. The honest correlation was −0.05, essentially zero, with a p-value of 0.82. Pure noise. The gap between those two numbers is the most important thing in this lesson.

A p-value of 0.0 felt like certainty. It was an artifact of how we counted.

Why the p-value lied: overlapping windows

To test "weather today vs price in 90 days," each data point uses a 90-day-forward return. But today's 90-day window and tomorrow's share 89 of the same days. So consecutive data points are almost identical, not independent.

The p-value formula assumes every observation is independent. We fed it ~1,800 "observations" that were really only about 20 independent windows. It happily reported near-certainty based on a sample 90× smaller than it thought. The statistics weren't wrong; we lied to them about the sample size.

false positive
A signal that looks real on your data but is actually chance. The default outcome of most searches, you find them by accident constantly.
overlapping windows
When consecutive observations share most of their data (like rolling forward-returns). It makes points look independent when they aren't, and inflates significance.
p-value
Roughly: the chance of seeing a result this strong if there were truly no signal. Low = "probably real", but ONLY if your observations are actually independent.
out-of-sample / non-overlapping test
The fix: sample one point every 90 days so the windows don't overlap. Fewer points, but an honest p-value you can trust.

The discipline that kills it

The defense is not cleverness, it's a checklist you run every single time:

Before you believe any signal

1. Confirm it out-of-sample, on data you never touched while searching.
2. Use independent observations (no overlapping windows) for the real p-value.
3. Count your tries. If you scanned 18 things, expect ~1 to look "significant" by chance, so one winner means nothing.
4. Demand the result make economic sense AND survive a fresh test. Either alone is not enough.

The honest ending

We tested 18 weather-to-commodity pairs. Zero survived the honest test. That feels like failure, but it's the opposite: the method worked. It stopped us from trading a mirage. Finding nothing, and knowing it, beats finding a "signal" that quietly loses money.

Your backtest shows a signal with p = 0.001 using 90-day rolling forward returns. What's your first move?

Most signals are mirages.

The skill isn't finding them, it's the discipline to disbelieve them until they survive an honest test. That's the whole course, in one habit.
