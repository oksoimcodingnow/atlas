The random walk under everything.

A stock price doesn't move smoothly — it jitters. Model that jitter as geometric Brownian motion: a steady drift μ plus random noise scaled by volatility σ. Every path below starts at the same price and obeys the same rule, yet they all end up somewhere different. That randomness is exactly what option pricing has to tame.

Start price fixed at 100. Re-roll a few times: the average end stays near theory, but any single path can wander far.
Why prices look the way they do. Each tiny step multiplies the price by 1 + μ·dt + σ·√dt·Z where Z is a fresh standard-normal shock. The drift nudges it up steadily; the noise term is scaled by √dt — and that square-root is the whole secret of finance. Uncertainty grows with the square root of time, not linearly: the ±1σ cone widens as √t, which is why a 4× longer horizon is only 2× as uncertain.
Sₜ₊₁ = Sₜ · exp( (μ − ½σ²)·dt + σ·√dt·Z ), Z ~ N(0,1)
Expected value grows as E[Sₜ] = S₀·e^{μt} (the gold line). The spread around it grows as σ·√t (the green cone). Black-Scholes prices an option by integrating over all these possible paths.
